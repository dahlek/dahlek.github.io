Emma Dahl, PhD

Weclome to my personal website!

I'm currently a postdoctoral scholar in Caltech's Geological and Planetary Sciences Department studying the chemistry and structure of protoplanetary disks. Most recently, I was a JPL postdoc studying Jupiter's clouds and helping coordinate Earth-based observational support for the Juno mission. The majority of my time is spent using world-class telescopes to observe disks and planets, and running radiative transfer and slab models with those data to better understand disk chemistry/structure and planetary meteorology; I also dabble in a variety of different projects and service roles.

Feel free to browse my website and don't hesitate to shoot me an email if you'd like to get in touch.

Interests & Expertise

Observational Astronomy

One of my core interests is in the methods of observational astronomy, including the development of instrumentation, the nitty-gritty of calibration pipelines, and the analysis of the data itself. I love following photons from their source all the way to pixel brightness on our screens. As both PI and co-I, I have won and been a part of observing time on telescopes such as NASA IRTF, Apache Point Observatory's 3.5-m telescope, Keck/NIRSPEC, JWST, and ALMA.

Planetary Science

I primarily study Gas and Ice Giant atmospheres, their weather layers, circulation patterns, colors, and how all these properties are tied to planetary meteorology and bulk characterstics. A long-term goal has been the identification of Jupiter's chromophore and its relationship to dynamical timescales.

Protoplanetary Disks

Most recently I have delved into the studies of the innermost regions of protoplanetary disks, where near-IR observations can illuminate these crades of planetary formation through the way in which CO gas (a main carbon carrier) varies with radius.

Outreach & Mentorship

A highlight of my career so far has been the opportunities to work with students and the public. I take my mentor responsibilities seriously and work hard to make sure each intern or student I've worked with becomes a better scientist over the time they work with me.
